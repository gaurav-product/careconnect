# Part A · Primary research package

## A note on how these guides are written

Every question asks about **behaviour that already happened**, because stated preference
about an unbuilt product is close to worthless. Two rules were applied throughout:

- **No product mentioned until the last five minutes.** If CareConnect comes up early,
  everything after it is a reaction to my idea rather than a description of their life.
- **No question that contains its own answer.** *"Would it help to know whether the
  medicine was given?"* is not research. *"Walk me through yesterday evening"* is.

Discarded examples, kept here so the bar is visible:

| Rejected question | Why | Replacement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Would you use an app that shows you the daily care record?" | Leading, hypothetical, socially easy to say yes to | "How did you find out how yesterday went?" |
| "Is trust a problem with attendants?" | Supplies the frame | "Tell me about the last time you had to replace someone." |
| "Would you pay ₹399 a month for this?" | Hypothetical spend | "What did you pay last month, and for what exactly?" |
| "Do you feel guilty being far away?" | Puts words in their mouth | "What's the hardest part of the week for you?" |

## A1 · Family interview guide (45 minutes)

**Recruit:** an adult child whose parent was discharged from hospital in the last 60 days
and who arranged paid home help. **Screen out:** anyone who did the caring themselves with
no paid attendant (different product, doc 05 anti-personas).

**Opening (5 min)** — "I'm trying to understand how families manage care at home after a
hospital stay. There's nothing to test and no right answers. Can I record audio? You can
stop at any point, and I'll delete anything you'd rather I didn't keep."

**The episode (15 min)**
1. Take me back to the day your father/mother was discharged. What happened from the
   moment the doctor said they could go home?
2. Who decided what would happen next, and how did that conversation go?
3. How did you find the person who is with them now? Walk me through it — the first call
   to the day they arrived.
4. How long did that take? What was the hardest part of it?
5. What did the hospital give you when you left? What did you do with it?

**The current routine (10 min)**
6. Yesterday — how did you find out how the day went? Who did you speak to and when?
7. Tell me about the last time you thought something was wrong. How did you find out?
8. What do you actually check on, and how? Show me on your phone if it's easier.
9. When something needs deciding — a fever, a change in medicine — who decides and how
   does that reach the person in the room?

**Breakdowns (10 min)**
10. Tell me about the last time the attendant didn't come, or left.
11. What happened to everything they knew about your parent when they left?
12. What's gone wrong that you only found out about later?
13. What are you paying, and for what exactly? Has that changed since the discharge?
14. If you could hand one part of this to someone else tomorrow, what would it be?

**Only now, the product (5 min)** — show the MVP for two minutes.
15. What would this have changed in the last two weeks, if anything? What would it not
    have changed?

**Signals to code afterwards:** unprompted mentions of finding vs verifying vs continuity;
who they call first when worried; what they already pay for; the words they use for the
attendant; whether the parent under-reports.

## A2 · Attendant / caregiver interview guide (35 minutes)

**Recruit:** working patient attendants, through an agency and independently. Conduct in
Hindi. **Pay for their time** — an hour of an attendant's day has a real price, and unpaid
research from a lower-power participant is coercive.

**Framing** — "I'm not from your agency and nothing you say reaches them. I want to
understand how the work actually goes, including the parts that are difficult."

**The shift (10 min)**
1. Walk me through your last shift, from arriving to leaving.
2. How do you know what you're supposed to do for this patient? Who told you, and when?
3. What did you do the first day with a new patient? How long before you felt you knew
   what was needed?

**Reporting, as it exists today (10 min)**
4. Who do you tell about the day, and when? What do you tell them?
5. Do you write anything down? Can I see it? (Ask to photograph any register or notebook.)
6. What does the family ask you about most?
7. Tell me about a time you had to tell a family something they didn't want to hear.

**The phone (5 min)**
8. Show me your phone — which apps do you use during a shift?
9. Is this phone yours alone? Does anyone else use it?
10. What happens when the network goes?

**Friction and incentives (10 min)**
11. What's the most annoying part of the job that isn't about the patient?
12. Has a family ever blamed you for something that wasn't your fault? What happened?
13. When you leave a patient and go to a new family, what do you carry with you?
14. If a family asked you to record what you did on a phone each shift, what would you
    think? What would make that worth doing? What would make you stop?
15. What would you not want the family to see?

**That last question is the important one.** A product that a worker experiences as
surveillance gets false data, and no amount of design fixes it. If several attendants name
the same thing, it comes out of the product.

## A3 · Hospital and agency interview guide (30 minutes)

**Recruit:** discharge coordinators or ward nurses at two hospitals; owners of two or three
small agencies (20–200 attendants).

**Discharge workflow (10 min)**
1. Walk me through a discharge for an elderly patient who'll need help at home.
2. What do you hand the family, and who explains it?
3. What do you do when a family asks you where to find an attendant?
4. What comes back to you in the first month after a discharge? What kinds of calls?

**Accountability and handover (10 min)**
5. How do you know whether a patient is doing what you told them to at home?
6. When an attendant changes at a patient's house, what happens to what they knew?
7. What documentation exists today? Who reads it?

**Distribution and threat (10 min — agencies)**
8. If a family showed you a screen that said your attendant missed a dose on Tuesday, what
   would you do?
9. Would you offer something like this to families? What would stop you?
10. What would you pay for, and what would you refuse to let attendants use?

Question 8 is deliberately confrontational because the conflict of interest identified in
doc 04 is the strategic question, and a polite version of it produces a polite answer.

## A4 · Observation protocol

Interviews describe intent; observation shows behaviour. One 45-minute session per
participant, in their own home or workplace, on their own phone.

**Rules for the observer**
- Say up front: *"I'm testing the product, not you. If something is confusing that is my
  mistake, and finding it is why we're here."*
- **Never coach.** No pointing, no "try the green one". If asked what to do, reply *"what
  would you do if I weren't here?"* and wait.
- Ask them to think aloud, then stay quiet. Count silently to ten before intervening.
- Intervene only when genuinely stuck for more than 60 seconds, and record it as a task
  failure, not a hint given.

**Family tasks** — set up a plan from a real (or realistic) discharge summary; invite an
attendant; find out what happened yesterday; deal with an alert; find what to give the next
attendant.

**Attendant tasks** — join with a code; start a shift; record a dose given; record a task
that could not be done; report something worrying; end the shift.

**Measure, per task:** completed unaided (yes/no) · time · errors · number of times they
asked for help · where their eyes went first · whether they read the bilingual labels in
Hindi or English.

**Instrumented alongside:** the analytics events in doc 13 give time-in-app and shift-close
rate for the same session, so self-report can be checked against behaviour.

## A5 · Consent and privacy for research

**Collect the minimum.** The research question is about workflow and coordination, not
about anyone's health. So:

- **Do not** record diagnoses, prescriptions, test results, or the patient's name.
  When a participant volunteers clinical detail, note the *shape* ("a post-surgical
  patient on a blood thinner"), never the specifics.
- **Do not** photograph documents containing a patient's name or hospital number. If a
  register is photographed, cover the identifying columns first.
- Use a participant code (F1, A1, H1) everywhere. Keep the one linking sheet offline and
  destroy it when the study closes.
- Recordings are transcribed, then the audio is deleted within 30 days.

**The patient is not a participant, and is not consenting.** Interviews are with the
family and the attendant, so the patient — an elderly person who may be unwell — is being
discussed without being asked. That is a real limitation of this research design, and it
carries into the product itself (doc 12, D-10). Where a patient is present and able,
tell them what is being studied and stop if they object.

**Consent script** — spoken, not a form to sign, because a form in this setting reads as
officialdom: *"This is research, not a service. Nothing you say goes to your agency / your
family / the hospital. I'll write about what I learn without your name. You can stop, skip
a question, or ask me to delete something at any point, including after we finish."*

**Attendants specifically:** confirm participation is voluntary and not arranged by their
employer, and pay them regardless of whether they complete the session.

# Part B · Validation experiments

Three uncertainties can kill this product. They are ordered by how cheaply they can do so.

## Experiment 1 · Attendant adoption *(the highest-risk test)*

**HYPOTHESIS** An attendant will record a full shift, unsupervised, in under five minutes,
and will keep doing it for two weeks.

**Why it comes first:** if this is false, every other question is irrelevant. The family's
entire value depends on a data source that does not pay us and gets nothing obvious in
return (doc 15, D-05).

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Participants** | 5 attendants across 3 families, recruited through one agency |
| **Duration** | 14 days of real use on the built MVP |
| **Setup** | Plans created with the family; attendants shown the app once, for two minutes, then left alone. No daily reminders from the researcher. |

**Measures (all instrumented today)**

| Measure | Source |
| --- | --- |
| Shift close rate | `shift_started` vs `shift_closed` |
| Items recorded ÷ items expected | day record score |
| Median time in app per shift | first to last event within a shift |
| Burst logging (all entries inside 5 min before close) | event timestamps |
| Correction rate | `care_entry_corrected` |
| Reason quality on not-done entries | manual read of the free text |
| Self-reported friction | short interview on day 3 and day 14 |

**PROPOSED THRESHOLDS — these are targets set in advance, not results.**

| Outcome | Proposed threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Pass | ≥ 80% shift close rate, median under 5 minutes, burst logging < 30% |
| Weak pass | ≥ 50% close rate, burst logging < 50% → cut the checklist to critical items only and rerun |
| Fail | < 50% close rate, or > 50% burst logging → the record is not trustworthy; the product needs a different data source, which is a different product |

**Expected learning** Whether the data source holds without coercion; which interactions
cost time; whether "could not do it" is used honestly or avoided.

**Ethics** Attendants are paid for participation. They are told exactly what the family
sees (the app says so on every shift screen). Nothing they record is shown to their agency
by us.

## Experiment 2 · Willingness to pay

**HYPOTHESIS** Families in the discharge week will pay for a documented record separately
from what they already pay the attendant.

**Why it matters:** H5 is the weakest link in the whole plan (doc 07), and the nearest
adjacent product is free (S17).

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Participants** | ~40 families reached through discharge desks and physiotherapist referrals |
| **Design** | Three cells: ₹399/month · ₹999 for the 30-day episode · free (control) |

**How the commitment test is run, and its limits**

A landing page explains the product honestly and ends at a price page. Choosing a paid
plan reaches a screen that says the product is in a limited pilot, offers a place on a real
waitlist, and **takes no money and no card details**. Everyone who joins the waitlist is
contacted.

- **No charging.** There is no payment flow, no consent to charge, and building one to run
  a test would be both premature and, without a proper refund and grievance process,
  unfair to a family in a crisis week.
- **A click is not revenue.** The strongest claim this design can support is *stated intent
  under mild commitment*. It will over-estimate real conversion, and the analysis must say
  so every time the number is quoted.
- A stronger follow-up, if the signal is positive: ask the waitlist to pay a refundable
  ₹100 to hold a pilot slot. That is a real commitment and needs a real refund path.

**Measures** page reach → price-page view → tier chosen → waitlist completed; open-text
reason for not choosing; which tier by segment (away child vs local).

**PROPOSED THRESHOLDS**

| Outcome | Proposed threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Pass | ≥ 30% of price-page viewers choose a paid tier |
| Fail | < 10% choose paid while the free tier converts strongly → the value is real but not purchasable alone; pivot to agency- or insurer-funded distribution |

**Expected learning** Whether reassurance is purchasable unbundled, and whether families
think in months or in episodes.

## Experiment 3 · Does the handover pack protect continuity?

**HYPOTHESIS** An incoming attendant who receives the handover pack becomes useful faster
than one who receives today's best case — a verbal briefing and the outgoing attendant's
last note.

**Why it matters:** this is the differentiator (doc 08). If it makes no difference, the
positioning is a story rather than a mechanism.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Participants** | 20 attendant changes observed inside the pilot |
| **Design** | Randomised at the moment of change. **Control:** the change happens as it does today — verbal briefing plus the last shift note. **Treatment:** the incoming attendant opens the full handover pack on their first screen. |

**Measures**

| Measure | How |
| --- | --- |
| Time to understand the care plan | Observed: minutes from first screen to first correctly recorded item |
| Missing information | Count of things the new attendant did not know that were in the plan (asked at end of first shift, from a fixed checklist) |
| Questions required | Number of clarifying calls or messages to the family in the first 72 hours |
| Task completion, first 72 hours | Items recorded ÷ items expected |
| Continuity recovery | Documented-day rate for 3 days after ÷ 3 days before the change |
| Family confidence | One question, same wording, day 3: "How confident are you that today went as planned?" 1–5 — **only if actually asked; otherwise not reported** |

**PROPOSED THRESHOLDS**

| Outcome | Proposed threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Pass | Continuity recovery ≥ 0.9 in treatment and materially lower in control; fewer clarifying questions |
| Fail | No difference → re-examine what actually restores continuity. It may be a phone call between the outgoing and incoming attendant, which the product should schedule rather than replace |

**Honest note on power:** 20 changes will not produce a statistically significant result.
This is a directional test designed to detect a large effect or its absence, and it must be
reported that way. A study powered to detect a small effect is not affordable at this stage
and would not change the decision.
