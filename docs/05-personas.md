# 05 · Personas

These are **composites built from secondary sources**, not from interviews. Everything
attributed to them traces to doc 03; nothing here is a quote from a real person, and no
name, number or "typical day" should be read as research output.

## Primary persona — Ananya, the daughter who is not in the room

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Age / role** | 41, works in Bengaluru; parents in Delhi |
| **The patient** | Her father, 74, discharged after a hip replacement |
| **What she pays** | ₹35,000–50,000/month for a live-in attendant, out of pocket (S8) |
| **Devices** | iPhone; checks in the gaps between meetings and after 10pm |
| **Who else is involved** | A brother in Gurugram who can reach the house in 40 minutes; the surgeon, reachable on WhatsApp; an agency coordinator who answers eventually |

**What she is actually trying to do:** know that today went right, and be told fast when
it did not — without becoming the person who calls her father four times a day to check
whether he took a tablet.

**How she copes today**
- Calls her father in the evening; he says everything is fine because he does not want to
  worry her (OBSERVATION).
- Calls the attendant, who reports verbally; both of them describe the same day
  differently.
- Asks her brother to drop in on Sundays.
- Screenshots the discharge summary into a WhatsApp group nobody scrolls back through.

**What goes wrong**
- She finds out about the missed antibiotic days later, or never.
- When the attendant leaves at short notice, everything she taught that person is gone
  and she re-teaches it over a phone call from another city (S9).
- She cannot answer the surgeon's question — *"has he been walking twice a day?"* — with
  anything better than "I think so".

**What she will pay for:** not more caregiving. **Proof, and early warning.**

**What would make her quit the product:** two false alarms in a week, or an attendant who
stops logging so that the app tells her nothing while looking like it should.

## Primary persona — Reena, the attendant

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Age / role** | 34, patient attendant placed by a local agency |
| **Work pattern** | 12-hour shifts, sometimes 24-hour live-in; rotated between cases |
| **Device** | A ₹9,000 Android phone, sometimes shared; data is limited |
| **Language** | Hindi first; reads English slowly; comfortable with WhatsApp voice notes |
| **Training** | Some agency training; competence varies widely across the market (S4, S14) |

**What she is trying to do:** get through the shift, keep the patient comfortable, avoid
being blamed for things outside her control, and be asked back.

**Her day already contains the reporting.** Good practice in this market already expects
a daily summary to the family, a written medication list, and a named escalation contact
(S21). Today she does it verbally, from memory, at the end of a twelve-hour shift.

**What goes wrong for her**
- She notices something worrying and has no clean way to escalate. Telling the elderly
  patient's son "I think the wound looks bad" over a phone call is a risk to her job.
- She is blamed for a missed dose that happened because the strip ran out and nobody
  bought more.
- She does everything right for three weeks and carries none of it to the next family.

**What she needs from the interface:** to be finished in under five minutes; to be able to
say *"I could not do this, and here is why"* without it being treated as a failure; and to
have "I told the family" be a fact on record rather than her word against theirs.

**What would make her stop using it:** a form that takes fifteen minutes, or any sense
that it exists to catch her out.

## Secondary persona — Ramesh, the patient

74, nine days after a hip replacement. Wants to be independent, hates being fussed over,
under-reports pain to his daughter and over-reports progress to his surgeon. Does not use
apps; does not want to.

**Design consequence:** the patient is **not a user** of the MVP. He is the subject of the
record, which raises a dignity and consent question the product must answer honestly
(doc 12, D-10) rather than design around.

## Anti-personas — people this product is explicitly not for

- **The family who cannot afford paid help.** With no attendant, there is nobody to log,
  and a checklist aimed at an exhausted daughter-in-law doing the care herself is a
  different product with a different value proposition (see doc 08, non-goals).
- **The elder living alone with no paid support.** This is a monitoring and companionship
  problem; the sources are clear that app adoption by Hindi-first seniors is the binding
  constraint (S18).
- **The hospital wanting a discharge-follow-up tool.** A plausible future buyer, and a
  completely different sale, product surface and compliance posture.
- **The agency wanting workforce management.** Also plausible later; building for them
  first would make the family the product rather than the customer, and would destroy the
  one thing that makes the record credible: that it does not belong to the agency.
