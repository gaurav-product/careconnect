# 01 · Problem discovery

## The brief, and what happened to it

The starting concept was *"a digital platform helping families discover, evaluate, book
and manage home caregivers."* That is a marketplace. It assumes the scarce thing is
**supply discovery**.

Discovery is where I started looking, and it is the first place the evidence stopped
agreeing with the brief. This document walks the discovery in the order it happened.

## 1. The demand is real and it is growing fast

- **FACT** India's 60+ population is projected to reach about **230 million by 2036**
  and to exceed the child population by **2046**, reaching roughly **20% of the
  population by 2050**. The 80+ group — the group that actually needs hands-on care —
  is projected to grow **279% between 2022 and 2050**. (S1, S2)
- **FACT** **12.2%** of Indians aged 60+ have supportive or palliative care needs, rising
  to about 16% in Uttar Pradesh and Bihar. (S3, citing a 2024 nationally representative
  study)
- **FACT** The India home healthcare market was about **USD 6.4bn in 2025**, growing at
  roughly **12.9% CAGR**, with services at 84% of revenue. (S4)
- **FACT** Over **40% of the elderly are in the poorest wealth quintile** and **18.7%
  have no income of their own**; **70% depend on family** for daily maintenance and
  **78% have no pension**. (S1, S5)

That last figure is the one that shapes everything downstream. The person receiving care
is usually not the person paying for it. **The buyer is the adult child.**

## 2. The state has a policy and not much delivery

- **FACT** NPHCE geriatric services now formally cover all **713 health districts**; a
  2019 amendment mandates home-care services for seniors with impairments; Elderline
  (14567) exists nationally; PMJAY was extended in Oct 2024 to cover 70+ regardless of
  income. (S2)
- **FACT** Delivery is uneven. In Nov 2025 Elderline in Uttar Pradesh answered **4,190 of
  28,643 calls**; Punjab under 3,000 of ~18,000. Home-based palliative visits ranged from
  ~650,000 in Kerala over eight months to services in **six districts** of Bihar. A
  public-health researcher's summary: *"Services exist on paper. Their implementation is
  lagging."* (S3)

**OBSERVATION** Public provision is not the substitute good. Families buy privately, out
of pocket, in a market with **no dedicated national regulatory framework** for home care
(S4).

## 3. Discovery is not scarce — and that is the finding that killed the brief

Counting what an Indian family in a metro can reach within 48 hours of a discharge:

| Channel | Availability | What it costs the family |
| --- | --- | --- |
| National providers (Portea, Care24, Apollo Home, HCAH) | Same-day to 24h in metros; Care24 advertises **3-hour allocation** and 2,000+ caregivers (S14) | Highest price, most process |
| Local agencies | Every hospital neighbourhood has several | Opaque, cash-heavy |
| Hospital discharge desk referral | Offered at the bedside | Zero search cost, zero vetting |
| Domestic-worker and neighbour networks, WhatsApp groups | Immediate | Zero search cost, zero accountability (S9) |
| Subscription eldercare (Samarth, Emoha, Anvayaa) | 40–350+ cities (S10) | Monthly fee on top |

**FACT** Care24 alone claims 2,000+ caregivers across ten cities with a three-hour
allocation promise (S14). **OBSERVATION** Across every source read, no family describes
being unable to *find* an attendant. They describe what happens *after* they find one.

## 4. What families actually describe going wrong

Sorted by how often it recurred across sources, with grade attached:

1. **The person sent is not the person promised.** *"Staff is extremely unprofessional",
   attendants "not adequately trained"* (D, S13). *"No experienced staff available"* on
   the agreed date (D, S13).
2. **The person leaves, and everything resets.** Rotation with no written handover;
   switching agencies means care "effectively resets to Day 1"; a leave gap leaves the
   family "untrained, unprepared, and anxious" (C, S16). Informal helpers "disappear
   without notice", triggering "a scramble of phone calls to relatives, neighbours, and
   WhatsApp groups" (C, S9).
3. **Nobody can see whether care actually happened.** *"No contracts, background checks,
   or formal oversight exist in many arrangements"*; problems like missing cash *"often
   go unnoticed for months"* (C, S9). Informal hiring offers *"no accountability
   structure, no replacement guarantee"* (C, S15).
4. **Medicines are the sharp edge.** Discharge changes the regimen, and reconciling old
   against new is the named first-week failure point (C, S19). Independently: **33.7%**
   of urban Indian older adults are on polypharmacy, **28.8%** on at least one
   potentially inappropriate medication, **19.7%** self-medicating (A, S6).
5. **The family caregiver is carrying it alone.** A review of 27 Indian studies finds
   burnout, and unmet needs for **information, training and respite** (A, S7).

## 5. Reframing the problem

The brief assumed a **search** problem. The evidence describes a **verification and
continuity** problem:

> A family can buy a body in the room within a day. What they cannot buy is confidence
> that the right care happened today, and a way to keep the plan intact when that body
> is replaced next week.

Two structural facts make this worse rather than better over time:

- **The workforce churns by design.** Agencies rotate staff, attendants take leave,
  live-in work is hard. Continuity is not the exception to plan for; it is the base case.
- **The knowledge lives in the attendant's head.** When they leave, the routine, the
  quirks, the doctor's last instruction and the reason for a change all leave with them.
  Nothing about the current market makes the care record belong to the patient.

## 6. Where the money and the anxiety concentrate

- **FACT** A live-in attendant runs **₹35,000–50,000 a month**; two 12-hour attendants
  **₹55,000–75,000**; nurse-led 24/7 care **₹70,000–1,20,000**; part-time **₹12,000–25,000**.
  Tier-2 cities run 15–20% lower. (C, S8 — a provider's own published bands, so treat as
  indicative)
- **OBSERVATION** A family already spending ₹35k–75k a month on a person in the room has
  demonstrated willingness to pay for care. The open question — the one this product
  exists to test — is whether they will pay separately for **proof** that the care
  happened.

## 7. Discovery conclusion

**Rejected:** caregiver discovery and booking as the core problem.
**Adopted:** post-placement accountability and continuity, at its sharpest in the
**first 30 days after a hospital discharge**, for a **family member who is not in the
room**.

Phases 2 and 3 test whether that reframing survives segmentation and the competitive
landscape. Phase 5 states it as falsifiable hypotheses.
